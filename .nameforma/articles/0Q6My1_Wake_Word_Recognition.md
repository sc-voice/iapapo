## Overview

Wake word recognition is a critical function for a Voice Assistant because wake words trigger chat state transitions.
For example, the transition from from IDLE to LISTENING is triggered by detection of the wake word.

Wake word recognition is typically handled on-device with a small and dedicated neural net trained to recognize the wake word directly from the audio signal samples rather than by scanning text generated by a general STT model.
The use of a local neural net minimizes recognition latency and provides quick recognition directly from the audio signal.

For a Personal Voice Assistant (PVA), there is also an interesting option to train the wake word recognizer directly in the user's own local environment.
Training positives can come from a chosen set of voices. The recognizer then favors those voices without identifying which speaker said the phrase.
In other words, PVAs may permit the use of highly personalized wake-words.

For this document, the wake word will be "IaPapo" (ee-yah-PAH-poh).

## Options

### Text scan of the transcript

The naive approach: search the transcribed text for "IaPapo". Drawbacks:
* Depends on the recognizer producing the exact spelling. Unusual names are misrecognized (the article's "Chaat" for "CHAT"), and the normalization map only patches known cases.
* Requires full transcription running continuously in IDLE, with battery and privacy cost.
* Volatile results change as words arrive, so matches can flicker and need debouncing.
* Cannot separate speech directed at the agent from speech nearby, including the agent's own TTS.

### Acoustic keyword spotter

Classify the audio signal directly, as Apple's on-device "Hey Siri" trigger does (from memory; to verify against current Apple documentation). A small trained model runs continuously and hands off to full transcription only after the trigger fires.

Apple-native route: a Create ML sound classifier run through SoundAnalysis (`SNAudioStreamAnalyzer`, `SNClassifySoundRequest`), entirely on device.
Third-party route: a custom wake word from a toolkit such as Picovoice Porcupine (commercial) or openWakeWord (would need Core ML conversion).

Preferred first test: the Create ML route.

## Training Data

* **Positives:** "IaPapo" spoken by the enrolled user. Synthetic `say` utterances can bootstrap the model, but real recordings from the user's voice are needed for accuracy.
  * Three variants, with the case cueing intonation for the user. The recognizer ignores case; all three are positives:
    * "IaPapo": normal.
    * "IAPAPO!": loud, emphatic.
    * "IaPapo?": rising, question.
* **Negatives, general:** everyday speech, TV, other voices.
  * Three long, natural PVA requests read by the user, with no wake word: one happy, one sad, one neutral. These are the speech the device will actually hear, and they later double as PVA test prompts.
    * Sad (confirmed): "How should I express my condolences for a friend who recently lost a pet after a long illness"
    * Neutral (candidate): "What is the average annual rainfall in the Amazonian basin?"
    * Happy (candidate): "What's a good recipe for a birthday cake that doesn't need eggs?"
  * Cut into overlapping windows. Window length is set from the trimmed duration of the positives (speech onset to offset, not file length), so length can't separate the classes.
* **Negatives, self-echo:** audio of IaPapo's own speech, picked up by the mic while SPEAKING, must not be treated as user speech. Record the `say` clips through the device speakers and mic so negatives match the real acoustic path. Confirm `say` produces the voice the app speaks with. Include replies containing the wake word, so the model rejects the agent's voice even when it says its own name.
* **Embedded mentions:** "The quick IaPapo jumped over the lazy dog." Whether this is a positive or negative depends on what the trigger means (see Open Issues).

## Trade-offs

* **Enrollment.** A user-specific model needs recordings across the environments the iPad is used in. This is a setup step to design.
* **Speaker identity is out of scope.** The recognizer favors the enrolled voices but does not identify who spoke. The PVA stays a simple utility without per-person recognition.
* **Noise and the pause.** The article's 500ms wakePause depends on silence detection, which degrades in noise. The listen timer already resets on transcribed text rather than raw mic energy, which argues against energy-based gating here.

## Training Wizard

The wizard trains and tests the detector. It is not yet part of the PVA's main UX.

* **Entry point:** a button on the DevTab opens the wizard. Integration into the PVA's main UX comes later.
* **Scope:** the wizard produces and evaluates the detector only. The ChatState machine is unchanged, so IDLE does not advance on a detection yet.
* **Live detection:** the wizard runs the detector on the user's speech as they speak the sentences.
* **Feedback:** after each detection the user marks it correct or incorrect. This covers both misses and false triggers.
* **Failed attempts:** incorrect results are kept as training examples, so the user's misses improve the next model.

**Open:** number of sentences, training duration, and what the user sees while training runs.

## Decisions

* Acoustic keyword spotting over transcript scanning as the target design.
* Everything runs on device; recordings and models stay local.
* YAGNI: first run a minimal experiment before building a training pipeline. Say the same sentences containing "IaPapo" yourself and through `say`, count triggers per source, and see whether the voice is a usable signal.

## Open Issues

* Does the trigger mean the token alone, or the token followed by a pause? Embedded mentions are positives under the first and negatives under the second.
* Pause-based gating in noise: drop the requirement and learn the token, or keep a pause as a learned boundary.
* Whether to require the wake word twice for noise robustness.
* Create ML versus a third-party engine.
* Whether the agent should respond only to the enrolled user.

## Glossary

* Keyword spotter: a classifier that detects a fixed word or phrase in audio, without full transcription.
* SoundAnalysis: Apple framework for classifying audio streams with Core ML models.
* Self-echo: the agent's own TTS output being captured by the mic as user speech.
